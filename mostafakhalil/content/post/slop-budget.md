## Cavemen Against Meat Proxies

I find the sentiment split around AI usage in work more personal than technical. Two factions are pointing pitchforks at each other. One thinks the other is a bunch of unproductive cavemen still living in the Stone Age, while the presumed cavemen think the other is a bunch of [meat proxies](https://meatproxy.me/test) who deliver nothing but _slop_. The reason it feels personal is that I see both factions arguing in absolutes. They treat _quality_ and _velocity_ as binary, something you either have or you don't, rather than a spectrum where you should consciously choose what to aim for. That target depends on many technical and economic factors. It can also change from one product to another, or even over time for the same product.

It is undeniable that, in most cases, unreviewed (or carelessly reviewed) AI outputs are worse than diligently reviewed or human-authored ones. And I'm not speaking only about code. Textual communication mediums for example suffer from this greatly. Receiving an 8-page verbose design document filled with buzzwords and overly technical prose that could have been a single paragraph makes me want to pluck my eyes out. More often than not, these documents contain subtly false statements that are not backed by proof and are hard to spot. This shifts the burden of the work from the author to the reviewer.

It is also undeniable that, in most cases, AI-assisted output is produced faster than human-authored or diligently reviewed work. A person who's using AI more and reviewing less will ship more. Even if you hate to admit it or have concerns about the quality produced, that turbo-speed shipping can have tremendous value in certain environments. It can be the deciding factor in whether a company survives or fails, and it should not be brushed off as pure, non-valueable slop.

Neither group produces a pragmatic argument. It always reads like someone is arguing for their self-worth and defending their identity, rather than measuring the net value their work style produces overall (yes, net, since neither comes for free).

## Google SRE Figured It Out Long Ago

Different software engineering domains have always required different levels of attention to certain details. Some people do, in fact, need to read the assembly generated by compilers, software whose output is deterministic and has long been trusted. They need to optimize around it in domains like aerospace engineering or systems development[^1].

The same goes for reliability in distributed systems. Not all systems need to run on [five nines of availability](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/configurepricequote/10.0.0?topic=principles-9s). It is OK for some systems to be down for hours. It might even be OK for some systems to lack data consistency for a while[^2]. And it can be more economically worthwhile, or existential, for some companies to ship faster even if it means less overall reliability.

Humans did not write perfect software before AI; they made tradeoffs based on business requirements and cost. And sometimes they unconsciously produced imperfect software simply due to a lack of knowledge, competence, or experience (if you think you're excluded, please read your code from a year ago). AI does not change that.

The quality spectrum is now wider. People are more susceptible to producing worse (and also better!) outputs with AI. We just tend to refer to the worse end of the spectrum now as _"slop"_, and lower quality has become more common, more socially accepted, and even indirectly encouraged or celebrated by a new wave of "entrepreneurs" and "investors."

I'm particularly interested in one concept that Google Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) introduced: the **[Error Budget](https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/)**. Google figured out long ago that building 100% reliable systems has a cost, and this cost is simply not worth paying all the time. Instead, they define a target for how reliable a system should be. That target differs from one product to another and is treated as a formal contract between developers working on product features and SREs trying to keep the system up and running.

The Error Budget is the compass that tells the team whether to push more features faster or to slow down and invest more in reliability. If your monthly target is 99% availability (~7.2 hours of downtime in a 30-day month) and you are sitting at 99.999% on day 25, you have spent almost none of your budget, so you can go ahead and push that feature faster!

## Read The Room

I see slop in the same way. Not all systems (or more generally, areas of work) require 0 slop, and, at the same time, some actually do! Depending on the historical consequences of my actions, I can increase or decrease my due diligence in reviewing AI outputs accordingly.

Remember that the main goal of using AI is to increase overall _productivity_[^3] over a period of time and across N number of people, with emphasis on both "a period of time" and "N number of people." For example, if you can file a PR in 1/10th the time today, but it takes 3x more review cycles, causes an incident and a revert, and makes it slower to add features in the future, you have an illusion of productivity, not an actual one.

I treat that like a dial. If I'm working on a mission-critical component, or sending a PR to someone who lives in a different timezone (making review cycles more expensive), or if I've recently been getting too many comments from a peer, I will slow down and put in more due diligence.

In contrast, if I'm sending a PR to a teammate who sits across from me, where we have a strong enough trust budget that they would be comfortable just giving me direct feedback, or I'm working on a system with loose requirements, I will be a _shipping cannon_ and might, for example, invest more in testing than reading every line of code.

You can look at the number of review comments you receive on your PRs and documents, code reverts, or the overall time to land a feature rather than just the time to file a PR. This information should be directly available with simple queries and can even be graphed on a dashboard! If you see an increase, maybe it's time to put more effort into your outputs. If it's flat or decreasing, maybe you have the budget to experiment with moving faster.

Direct feedback from teammates is also a very strong, if not the strongest, indicator, as long as you actually foster the environment where people are comfortable giving you that feedback.

You do not need to put yourself in either box: the 10x agentic engineer or the anti-AI quality police. You can always move in either direction!

## Summary

Slop is not a scary beast that we must kill completely, nor is it so benign that we shouldn't care about managing it. Similar to how Google SRE embraces risk rather than eliminating it by defining SLOs and error budgets, track your own slop budget and adjust accordingly. If your outputs are of high quality, maybe you can afford to experiment with moving faster with AI. If your teammates are side-eyeing you or you have been causing outages, maybe it's time to slow down a bit. You will also need to adjust based on the criticality of the work you're doing. 

[^1]: Linus Torvalds even said in 2012, when compilers were already sufficiently advanced, that he cared about machine code (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-xbzGngfQEw).

[^2]: I once saw a system with a concurrency issue that triggered once every few months, causing certain data to become inconsistent. Investing in (1) proving it was rare enough and would always remain rare, (2) a detection and alerting mechanism, and (3) a manual recovery mechanism was a lot more economically viable than trying to make the bug impossible. Imagine suggesting this to banking software or a trading platform.

[^3]: In the sense that you are using AI to assist in doing the work you were previously doing without AI, not when AI is, for example, your whole product.
