Cavemen Against Meat Proxies#

When it comes to sentiment around AI-assisted development, people are split between two factions pointing pitchforks at each other.

Some firmly believe that code is now obsolete, cheap to produce, and no longer worth a human looking at. We should completely shift our efforts into verification methods at a higher level of abstraction instead of manually reviewing code. This faction usually compares code produced by AI to assembly or machine code produced by a compiler, arguing that we trust the compiler enough not to read the machine code.

Others say that overtrusting AI more often than not produces code that does not pass the quality bar, resulting in a plethora of operational and maintenance issues. That subpar work is often referred to as slop. This faction often takes offense when reviewing AI-generated outputs. On top of the system issues that slop creates, it shifts the burden of the work from authors to reviewers, which makes the disagreement in sentiment more personal than technical.

This also applies to artifacts beyond code. Textual communication like design documents and PR comments suffer from the same sentiment split, if not worse. AI enables people to research and produce designs in areas that would otherwise have taken them weeks to onboard to, but it tends to generate lengthy, verbose artifacts filled with statements not backed up by proof, making the recipient want to pluck their eyes out sometimes.

While the former will accuse the latter of being cavemen who live in the Stone Age, and the latter will accuse the former of being meat proxies who produce no valuable work, the truth is - as usual - somewhere in between.

Google SRE Figured It Out Long Ago#

Different software engineering domains have always required different levels of attention to certain details. Some people do, in fact, need to read the assembly generated by compilers and optimize around it in domains like aerospace engineering or systems development 1.

The same goes for reliability in distributed systems. Not all systems need to run on five nines of availability. It is OK for some systems to be down for hours. It might even be OK for some systems to lack data consistency for a while 2.

Humans did not write perfect software before AI; they made tradeoffs based on business requirements and cost. And sometimes they unconsciously produced imperfect software simply due to a lack of knowledge, competence, or experience (if you think you’re excluded, please read your code from a year ago). AI does not change that.

The quality spectrum is now wider. People are more susceptible to producing worse (and also better!) outputs with AI. People just tend to refer to the worse end of the spectrum now as “slop”, and lower quality has become more common, more socially accepted, and even indirectly encouraged or celebrated by a new wave of “entrepreneurs” and “investors.”

I’m particularly interested in one concept that Google SRE introduced: the Error Budget. Google figured out long ago that building 100% reliable systems has a cost, and this cost is simply not worth paying all the time. Instead, they define a target for how reliable a system should be. That target differs from one product to another and is treated as a formal contract between developers working on product features and SREs trying to keep the system up and running.

The Error Budget is the compass that tells the team whether to push more features faster or to slow down and invest more in reliability. If your target is 99% availability each month and on the 25th you are at 99.999%, go ahead and push that feature quicker, you have a budget of 0.999% to burn while still hitting your requirements.

I see slop in the same way. Depending on the target system, the people I’m working with, and the historical consequences of my actions, I either increase or decrease my due diligence in reviewing the AI outputs accordingly.

How It Extends to Slop#

So what does slop actually affect?

  1. Peer humans: people who are sick of bearing more of the review burden than the person who produced the work.

  2. Systems: the negative impact on existing quality metrics, such as performance, reliability, correctness, and ongoing maintenance costs due to a lack of due diligence.

If I’m working on a mission-critical component, or sending a PR to someone who isn’t on my team and maybe lives in a different timezone (making review cycles more expensive), or if I’ve recently been getting too many comments or direct feedback from a peer, I will slow down and read every line of the output.

In contrast, if I’m sending a PR to a teammate who sits across from me, where we have a good enough relationship and trust budget that they would be comfortable just giving me direct feedback, or I’m working on a system with loose requirements, I will be a shipping cannon and might invest more in testing than reading every line of code.

Similar to how you look at availability and latency when measuring reliability, when tracking slop, you can look at the number of review comments you receive on your PRs and documents, outages caused, code reverts, or the overall time to submit a PR rather than just the time to create one. This information should be directly available with simple queries and can even be graphed on a dashboard! If you see an increase, maybe it’s time to put more effort into your outputs. If it’s flat or decreasing, maybe you have the budget to experiment with moving faster.

Direct feedback from teammates is also a very strong indicator, as long as you foster an environment where people are comfortable giving you that feedback. You do not need to put yourself in either box: the 10x agentic engineer or the anti-AI quality police. You can always move in either direction!

Summary#

Slop is not a scary beast that we must kill completely, nor is it so benign that we shouldn’t care about managing it. Similar to how Google SRE embraces risk rather than eliminating it by defining SLOs and error budgets, track your own slop budget and adjust accordingly. If your outputs are of high quality, maybe you can afford to experiment with moving faster with AI. If your teammates are side-eyeing you or you have been causing outages, maybe it’s time to slow down a bit. You will also need to adjust based on the criticality of the work you’re doing.
